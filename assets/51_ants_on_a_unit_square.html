Geometric Pigeonhole Principle

51 Ants on a Unit Square

Prove that no matter how 51 ants are placed in a unit square, a disk of radius 1/7 can always be positioned to cover at least 3 ants.

1. Divide the square into 25 cells

Split the unit square into a 5 × 5 grid. Then each small square has side length 1/5.

2. Apply the pigeonhole principle

If each of the 25 cells contained at most 2 ants, then the total number of ants would be at most:

25 × 2 = 50

But there are 51 ants, so at least one cell must contain 3 or more ants.

3. Show one disk covers that entire cell

Place the center of the glass disk at the center of the crowded small square. The farthest points in the cell are the corners.

needed radius = √[(1/10)² + (1/10)²] = √2 / 10 ≈ 0.14142
glass radius = 1 / 7 ≈ 0.14286
√2 / 10 < 1 / 7

So the radius-1/7 disk covers the whole small square, and therefore covers all ants in that square — at least 3 ants.

Conclusion

No matter how the 51 ants are arranged, there is always a placement of the radius-1/7 disk that covers at least 3 ants.

Why the “50 + 1” arrangement is the key idea

The tight example puts exactly 2 ants in each of the 25 cells, for a total of 50 ants. The 51st ant must land in some existing cell, forcing that cell to contain 3 ants. Since the disk covers the whole cell, it covers those 3 ants regardless of their exact locations.
